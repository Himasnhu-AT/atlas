Retrieval needs a way to decide that a question and a document are about the same thing, without
requiring that either one contains the other's words. That is what embeddings are for, and the
machinery underneath is smaller than the mystique around it.

## What an embedding is

An embedding model is a function from text to a fixed length list of floats. The same model maps
a three word query and a three hundred word document to vectors of exactly the same size, which
is the property that lets you compare them at all.

The vectors are not arbitrary. The model is trained so that texts people treat as related end up
close together and everything else ends up far apart, and position in the space carries meaning
as a side effect of that objective. That is the whole of "distance means meaning": not a property
of vectors, a property this particular training produced.

Two consequences matter. Individual dimensions mean nothing, there is no "refunds" axis, so the
only sensible operation is comparing one vector to another. And a vector is comparable only to
vectors from the same model, which is why the query is embedded with the same client as the
documents, and why changing embedding model means re-embedding the whole corpus.

## Why cosine, and why it is a dot product

Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors and ignores how long they are.

```python
def cosine_similarity(a, b):
    dot = sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
    return dot / (math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a)) * math.sqrt(sum(y * y for y in b)))
```

Ignoring length is the entire point. Magnitude mostly tracks how much text there was rather than
what it was about, so a long document and a short one on the same subject should not be pushed
apart for that reason alone.

Most embedding APIs return vectors already normalised to length one, and `mockllm.embed_text`
does the same. For unit vectors both square roots are 1, so the computation collapses to the dot
product. Keep the division anyway: it costs nothing and it keeps the function correct if you ever
hand it vectors from somewhere that does not normalise.

Euclidean distance is the obvious alternative and on unit vectors it ranks identically, because
the two are related by a fixed formula when every vector has the same length. The choice only
matters when your vectors are not normalised, and then cosine is usually what you want.

Cosine runs from -1 to 1 and is not a percentage: 0.7 does not mean 70 percent relevant, it means
an angle, and the only meaningful comparison is against other scores from the same query.

## What these embeddings can and cannot do

The embeddings in this course are real vectors, so every ranking you see is produced by
arithmetic on text rather than by a fixture. They are also not a trained model, and it is worth
knowing exactly where the line is.

`embed_text` uses the hashing trick: lowercase, split into words, hash each word into one of 256
buckets, count, normalise. Two texts score highly when they share words. That is all it knows.

Everything structural about retrieval survives that. Ranking, thresholds, top `k`, the shape of
the code, and every design decision in this module work identically against a trained embedding
API.

What does not survive is paraphrase. Watch it fail on the corpus in the exercise, where
`billing-refunds` says refunds are issued within ten business days:

```
query: "how long until I get my money back"
   auth-sessions        0.2372
   billing-refunds      0.0000
   billing-refunds-eu   0.0000
```

The document that answers the question scores exactly zero, because "money back" and "refunds"
share no words. Meanwhile a document about session expiry scores 0.24 on the strength of "you",
"will" and "I". A trained model would put the refund document near the top, because it has seen
the two phrasings used in the same contexts a great many times.

What is worth learning from that is not the miss, it is what the code does about it. Swap in a
real embedding API and the miss goes away; the need for the floor does not, because there is
always some query your corpus cannot answer.

## A for loop, and what a vector store adds

Searching an index is one pass:

```python
query_vector = client.embeddings.create(input=query).data[0].embedding
scored = [
    (cosine_similarity(query_vector, vector), position, document)
    for position, (vector, document) in enumerate(zip(index.vectors, index.documents))
]
scored.sort(key=lambda item: (-item[0], item[1]))
```

That is brute force, it is exactly correct, and it is fast enough for thousands or tens of
thousands of chunks. Reaching for a database before you need one is the most common overbuild in
this whole area.

The sort key is not decoration. Scores descending, position ascending as the tie break. Near
duplicate documents are ordinary, and a retriever that orders ties differently between runs makes
every test above it flaky.

A vector store earns its place on four things: it persists the vectors, it supports updates and
deletes, it filters on metadata so you can search only this customer's documents, which is a
security boundary rather than a feature, and it offers approximate nearest neighbour search when
linear scanning becomes the bottleneck.

Approximate is the word to notice. Structures like HNSW graphs and IVF partitions examine a
fraction of the vectors, trading exactness for speed, so they can miss a genuine nearest
neighbour, and the miss looks exactly like a document that was never ingested. Worth taking on at
a scale where brute force is too slow, and a strange thing to take on before then.

## k, the floor, and admitting it is too close to call

Three separate decisions get collapsed into one by most retrieval code, and separating them is
most of what the exercise is about.

`k` is how many results you take, and it is a budget decision: how much context you can afford in
the prompt. Raising it raises recall, the share of questions whose answer came back at all, and
lowers precision, the share of what came back that was worth having.

The threshold is what counts as a match. It is a quality decision, it moves precision and recall
the other way, and it is what lets retrieval return nothing:

```
query: "how do I reset my password"        query: "how long until I get my money back"
   auth-reset       0.3273                    auth-sessions     0.2372
   auth-sessions    0.1021                    billing-refunds   0.0000
```

Both queries have a top result and only one of them has a good one. Without a floor there is no
difference between the two situations, because there is always a least bad document, and handing
it to a model is how an unanswerable question becomes a confident wrong answer. At a floor of
0.25 the first query returns one document and the second returns none; drop the floor to 0.1 and
the second confidently returns a document about session expiry to a question about refunds.

There is no universal value. It depends on your embedding model, your corpus and what a wrong
answer costs you. Start somewhere, look at what it drops, and move it deliberately.

The third decision is the one most retrievers skip: saying out loud that the top results are too
close to call. Consider a question about invoices against the same corpus:

```
query: "invoices business days"
   billing-refunds      0.3086
   billing-refunds-eu   0.2981
   billing-invoices     0.2649
```

Three documents mention business days, the two refund documents beat the invoice document on
shared words alone, and the gap between first and second is 0.0105. The retriever has found
candidates and cannot choose between them, and the document the question was actually about is
third.

Comparing that gap to a minimum margin turns an invisible coin flip into a label the caller can
act on. Ambiguity is not a failure, and near duplicates are normal: two documents that both
answer the question is a fine outcome. The failure is picking one at random and presenting it as
the answer, which is what code without a margin check does on every ambiguous query, silently.

## Your exercise

Build the retrieval layer: `build_index`, `search`, `search_above`, and `assess`, returning a
`Verdict` labelled `CONFIDENT`, `AMBIGUOUS` or `NO_MATCH`.

The tests use the corpus above, so the numbers in this lesson are the numbers you will see. They
check that the whole corpus is embedded in one request rather than one per document, that ties
resolve toward the document added first, that a hit exactly on the threshold survives, and that
the paraphrased refund question reports `NO_MATCH` rather than answering from the session
document.

Two things catch people. `search` ranks and does not judge, so the threshold belongs only in
`search_above`; applying it in both makes `k` meaningless. And the margin is `None` for a single
hit rather than `0.0`, because a margin of zero reads as a dead heat and would report every
single-result query as ambiguous.
