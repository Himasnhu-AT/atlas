Documents get split because the chunk is the retrieval unit, and the retrieval unit is what
ends up in the prompt. Retrieve whole documents and a question about one sentence costs you a
forty page manual, most of which is noise the model has to read past. Retrieve single sentences
and you get a precise match to a fragment that means nothing without the paragraph around it.

Somewhere between those is a size that is small enough to be specific and large enough to be
self contained. Finding it is not a solved problem, and most of what makes retrieval good or
bad is decided here rather than in the choice of embedding model.

## Four ways to cut

| Strategy | Boundary | Preserves | Destroys |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed size | A character or token count | Nothing in particular | Sentences, paragraphs, tables, code blocks |
| Sentence or paragraph | Punctuation or a blank line | Complete thoughts | Anything spanning a paragraph, and produces wildly uneven sizes |
| Structural | Markdown headings, HTML elements, function definitions | The author's own organisation | Nothing, where the structure exists |
| Recursive | The coarsest separator that fits | Structure where there is any, degrades gracefully where there is not | Less than the others, which is why it is the default in most stacks |

Fixed size is worth writing once, because it is the baseline everything else is measured
against and because on genuinely unstructured text, a wall of scanned OCR with no paragraph
breaks, it is not much worse than anything cleverer.

Structural splitting is the best option when the structure is real. A Markdown document split
on headings gives chunks that correspond to what a human would call a section, and the heading
comes along for free as metadata. The catch is that it only works on documents that have
structure, and half of any real corpus does not.

Recursive splitting is the compromise that most stacks land on. Try to split on blank lines. If
a piece is still too big, split that piece on single newlines. Still too big, split on sentence
ends. Still too big, split on spaces. The important detail is in what you pass down:

```python
if len(piece) > max_size:
    chunks.extend(split_recursive(piece, max_size, rest))
```

`rest`, not the full separator list. Each level of the recursion has strictly fewer separators
to try, which is what makes it terminate. Pass the whole list down and a piece that the current
separator failed to break gets handed the same separator again, forever.

The other detail is the greedy rejoin. After splitting on blank lines you walk the pieces and
join consecutive ones back together while they still fit. Skip that step and a document of
short paragraphs becomes a list of one-line chunks, each too small to carry its own meaning,
and your index fills up with fragments that match queries and answer nothing.

## Overlap, and what it costs

Overlap repeats the tail of one chunk at the head of the next. It exists because a boundary
placed in the wrong spot loses the answer to both sides: the first chunk has the setup, the
second has the conclusion, and neither one matches a query for the whole thing.

```python
step = size - overlap
for start in range(0, len(text), step):
    windows.append(text[start : start + size])
```

The cost is not subtle. Ten percent overlap is ten percent more vectors, ten percent more
storage, and ten percent more of everything the index does. It also produces near duplicate
hits: a query that matches the overlapping region retrieves two chunks that are largely the
same text, which wastes part of your context budget on a repeat. If you are returning the top
three chunks and two of them are the same paragraph, you are effectively returning two.

Overlap matters most with fixed size splitting, because that is where boundaries land in
arbitrary places. With structure aware splitting the boundaries are already where a human would
have put them, and the case for overlap gets much weaker.

## The size argument

You will find "512 tokens" and "1000 characters with 200 overlap" quoted everywhere as though
they were settled. They are not settled, they are starting points, and the people quoting them
measured on a corpus that is not yours.

What is actually true is the direction of the tradeoff. Smaller chunks give more precise
matches and lose context, so the retrieved text is on topic and may not contain enough around
it to answer. Larger chunks carry their context and dilute the match, because a single vector
now has to represent several ideas at once and ends up representing none of them strongly.
Larger chunks also spend more of the prompt budget per hit, so top three at 2000 characters is a
different prompt from top three at 400.

The only way to settle it is to measure on your own queries. Write down twenty questions people
actually ask, note which document should answer each one, and check how often it comes back in
the top three at different chunk sizes. That is a morning of work and it will tell you more
than any blog post. It is also the same evaluation you will need later for everything else, so
it is not throwaway effort.

One practical asymmetry: it is easier to recover from chunks that are slightly too small than
slightly too large. A small chunk can be expanded at retrieval time by pulling its neighbours
from the document, since you know the index of each chunk. There is no equivalent trick for a
chunk that is too big.

## Metadata is what makes a citation possible

A chunk that does not know where it came from can be retrieved and put in a prompt. It cannot
be cited, and an answer you cannot trace is an answer nobody can check. So every chunk carries
its source document, its position, and whatever headings sat above it.

The headings do a second job. Chunk four of a pricing page might read, in full, "It costs $49
per month." Embedded on its own it matches queries about $49 and nothing about pricing, because
the words that would have matched are in a heading two hundred characters earlier. Prefixing
the headings before you embed fixes that, and it is the cheapest retrieval improvement
available:

```python
def with_context(self) -> str:
    parts = [p for p in (self.metadata.get("title"), self.metadata.get("section")) if p]
    if not parts:
        return self.text
    return f"{' > '.join(parts)}\n\n{self.text}"
```

Note that you embed the contextualised text and you display the raw text. Those are different
strings for a reason: the prefix is there to help the vector match, not to be quoted back at a
user as though the document said it.

Write `split_fixed`, `split_recursive`, and `chunk_document`, plus the two methods on `Chunk`
that turn a piece of text into something citable.

The tests check the size limit, the greedy rejoin, that a chunk boundary does not land inside
the price sentence when a blank line was available, and that no text is lost. They also check
the fixed splitter against the same document to show you the boundary landing in the wrong
place, which is the failure this whole lesson is about.

Two things catch people. The first is passing the full separator list into the recursive call
instead of the remaining one, which does not error, it hangs. The second is the overlap loop:
once a window reaches the end of the text you have to stop, because every later start produces
a suffix of a window you already emitted.
