Two assets can be highly correlated while drifting apart indefinitely over time. Trading a diverging spread based purely on correlation leads to catastrophic drawdowns.

## Correlation versus cointegration

**Correlation** measures the co-movement of periodic returns:

$$
\rho_{X, Y} = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_X, R_Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}
$$

Two stock prices can share a strong positive correlation of 0.90 while one doubles and the other loses half its value over a year. The ratio or difference between them wanders without bound.

**Cointegration** is a property of non-stationary price series. Two series $Y_t \sim I(1)$ and $X_t \sim I(1)$ are cointegrated if a linear combination exists that is stationary:

$$
e_t = Y_t - (\alpha + \beta X_t) \sim I(0)
$$

The coefficient $\beta$ is the **hedge ratio**: for every 1 share of $Y$ held long, the portfolio holds $\beta$ shares of $X$ short. The resulting portfolio equity $e_t$ mean-reverts around zero.

## OLS hedge ratio and residual z-score

To estimate the static hedge ratio between $Y$ and $X$:

$$
\beta = \frac{N \sum X Y - \sum X \sum Y}{N \sum X^2 - (\sum X)^2}, \quad \alpha = \bar{Y} - \beta \bar{X}
$$

The synthetic spread is the residual series:

$$
e_t = Y_t - (\alpha + \beta X_t)
$$

To generate actionable trading signals across instruments with different dollar price scales, the spread is normalized into a dimensionless **z-score**:

$$
Z_t = \frac{e_t - \mu_e}{\sigma_e}
$$

When $Z_t > +2.0$, $Y$ is overpriced relative to $X$, generating a short $Y$ / long $X$ signal. When $Z_t < -2.0$, $Y$ is underpriced relative to $X$, generating a long $Y$ / short $X$ signal.
