A stock or future has a linear payoff: gaining \$1 for every \$1 increase in asset price, and losing \$1 for every \$1 decline.

An **option** provides an asymmetric, non-linear payoff structure.

## European option payoffs

For an option with strike price $K$ expiring at time $T$:

A **European call option** grants the right, but not the obligation, to purchase the underlying asset at strike price $K$ at expiration:

$$
\text{Payoff}_{\text{call}} = \max(0, \; S_T - K)
$$

A **European put option** grants the right, but not the obligation, to sell the underlying asset at strike price $K$ at expiration:

$$
\text{Payoff}_{\text{put}} = \max(0, \; K - S_T)
$$

An option's total market price decomposes into two components:
1. **Intrinsic value**: The value if exercised immediately ($S - K$ for calls, $K - S$ for puts, bounded at zero).
2. **Time value (extrinsic value)**: The premium paid for the probability that the underlying asset moves further in-the-money before expiration.

## Put-call parity

For European options on non-dividend-paying assets with risk-free rate $r$ and time to maturity $T$, holding a long call and a short put with identical strike $K$ and maturity $T$ replicates a forward contract:

$$
C - P = S_0 - K e^{-r T}
$$

If market option prices violate this equation, an arbitrageur executes a synthetic conversion or reversal trade:
* If $C - P > S_0 - K e^{-r T}$: Sell the overpriced call, buy the underpriced put, buy the stock, and borrow $K e^{-r T}$ cash.
* If $C - P < S_0 - K e^{-r T}$: Buy the call, sell the put, short the stock, and lend $K e^{-r T}$ cash.

Both synthetic portfolios deliver a risk-free locked-in profit at expiration regardless of where the underlying stock price finishes.
