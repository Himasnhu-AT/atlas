Standard deviation treats upside gains and downside losses symmetrically. Institutional risk management focuses specifically on downside tail losses.

## Value at Risk (VaR)

**Value at Risk (VaR)** estimates the maximum expected loss over a specific horizon at a given confidence level $1 - \alpha$ (e.g. 95% or 99%):

$$
P(\text{Loss} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha) = 1 - \alpha
$$

A daily 95% VaR of 2.0% means that on 95 out of 100 trading days, the portfolio loss will not exceed 2.0%.

Two common estimation methods:
1. **Historical VaR**: Non-parametric percentile lookup from sorted historical returns.
2. **Parametric (Gaussian) VaR**: For mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$
\text{VaR}_\alpha = -(\mu + z_\alpha \sigma)
$$

Where $z_\alpha = \Phi^{-1}(\alpha)$ is the standard normal quantile (e.g. $z_{0.05} \approx -1.645$).

## The failure of VaR and Expected Shortfall (CVaR)

VaR provides no information about what happens on the 5 days out of 100 when losses breach the threshold. A portfolio could lose 2.1% or 50.0%; both count identically as a single VaR breach. Furthermore, VaR is not mathematically subadditive, meaning combining two portfolios can produce a VaR greater than the sum of their individual VaRs.

**Expected Shortfall (Conditional VaR / CVaR)** measures the expected loss conditional on the loss exceeding the VaR threshold:

$$
\text{CVaR}_\alpha = E[\text{Loss} \mid \text{Loss} \ge \text{VaR}_\alpha]
$$

In a Gaussian distribution:

$$
\text{CVaR}_\alpha = -\mu + \sigma \frac{\phi(z_\alpha)}{\alpha}
$$

Expected Shortfall is a coherent risk measure that penalizes fat tails.
