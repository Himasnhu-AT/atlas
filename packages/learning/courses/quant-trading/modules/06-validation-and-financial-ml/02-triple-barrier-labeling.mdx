      kind: assign
      prompt: >-
        Entry is at 100, the profit take sits at 102, the stop sits at 98, and the position
        expires after ten bars. Sort each path by the label the triple barrier gives it.
      buckets:
        - id: up
          label: Plus one
          note: The upper barrier was touched before the lower one and before the clock ran out.
        - id: down
          label: Minus one
          note: The lower barrier was touched first. The position was closed there whatever happened next.
        - id: flat
          label: Zero
          note: Ten bars passed with neither horizontal barrier touched, so the position was closed at market.
      items:
        - id: steady-rise
          label: Climbs through 102.4 on bar 4, then slides back to 99 by bar 10
          bucket: up
          because: The upper barrier was touched on bar 4 and the trade ended there. What the price did afterwards belongs to a position you no longer had.
        - id: dip-then-rally
          label: Drops to 97.6 on bar 2, then rallies all the way to 104 by bar 8
          bucket: down
          because: Down through 98 on bar 2 closes it at minus one. A fixed ten bar horizon reads the 104 close and calls this a winner, which is the single most common way a backtest invents profit.
        - id: chop
          label: Oscillates between 99.2 and 101.4 for all ten bars and closes at 100.9
          bucket: flat
          because: Neither barrier was reached, so the vertical one decided it. This is the label most paths get, and a labeller that never produces it is set too tight.
        - id: exact-touch
          label: Falls to exactly 98.00 on bar 6 and closes the session at 101
          bucket: down
          because: A touch is a touch. The barrier is a price a real stop order would have been filled at, so the label has to agree with the order.
        - id: late-breakout
          label: Reaches 101.9 on bar 9 and does not clear 102 until bar 11
          bucket: flat
          because: Bar 11 is past the vertical barrier and the position was already closed. A labeller that looks one bar too far turns this into a plus one and teaches the model to predict its own bug.
        - id: gap-up
          label: Gaps straight to 103 on bar 1 and stays there
          bucket: up
          because: The barrier was crossed rather than touched, so the fill is worse than 102. The label is still plus one, and the difference between the barrier and the fill is a cost the labels do not carry.
      hints:
        - Work along each path in time order and stop at the first barrier it reaches.
        - Two of these six end far away from where their label says, and those two are the point.
        - One path clears the upper barrier on bar 11, and the position was closed on bar 10.
      reveal: >-
        Fixed horizon labelling asks where the price ended. The triple barrier asks what
        happened on the way, which is the same question a risk system asks. Where the two
        disagree, the fixed horizon label is describing a position that would have been
        closed before the horizon arrived, and a model trained on it learns to hold through
        drawdowns it will never be allowed to hold through.

          note: Symmetric in percent is not symmetric in probability. A name that moves 6 percent a day touches a barrier on the first bar, and one that moves 0.3 percent will not touch either inside a month.
        - id: scale-by-vol

          correct: true
          note: This makes the barriers equally hard to reach across the universe, so a plus one means the same thing whichever name produced it. Labels that do not mean the same thing cannot be pooled into one training set.

          label: Widen both barriers to 5 percent so the quiet names can reach them
          note: The quiet names still will not, and the volatile ones now touch even sooner in relative terms. Moving a fixed threshold changes which names are mislabelled, not the fact that the threshold is fixed.

          label: Shorten the vertical barrier so more paths expire at zero

      reveal: >-
        Barriers are usually set as a multiple of a rolling daily volatility estimate, and
        the vertical barrier gets the same treatment by being counted in event time rather

Standard supervised machine learning in finance labels observations using fixed-time horizon returns: $y_t = \text{sign}(P_{t+h} - P_t)$.

Fixed-horizon labeling ignores intra-horizon path dynamics: a trade that moves within 0.1% of a stop loss before recovering at step $t+h$ receives a positive label, even though a real trader or automated risk rule would have been liquidated along the path.

## The three barriers

The **triple-barrier method** dynamically tracks price evolution across three simultaneous boundaries:

1. **Upper horizontal barrier (Profit Take)**: $P_{\text{upper}} = P_t \cdot (1 + \text{PT})$. If touched first, label $y_t = +1$.
2. **Lower horizontal barrier (Stop Loss)**: $P_{\text{lower}} = P_t \cdot (1 - \text{SL})$. If touched first, label $y_t = -1$.
3. **Vertical barrier (Time Expiration)**: $t + H$. If neither horizontal barrier is touched within $H$ steps, the position is closed at market, and label $y_t = 0$.

## Benefits of path-dependent labeling

* Labels reflect actual execution logic rather than idealized point-to-point measurements.
* The model learns to predict whether a setup provides sufficient momentum to hit target thresholds before triggering risk stops.
* It eliminates artificial noise from trades that wander aimlessly without reaching either statistical boundary.
