A model knows what was in its training data. That is a great deal, and none of it is yours. It
has not read your API reference, your ticket history, your architecture decisions, or the
config someone changed last Tuesday. Ask about any of them and you get an answer, because
producing plausible text is the whole job and there is no separate signal inside the model that
says "I have never encountered this".

That is the gap this module closes, and the interesting part is not that the gap exists. It is
that closing it badly is worse than leaving it open, because a wrong answer with a source
attached is harder to catch than a wrong answer without one.

## Three ways to get your data in front of the model

There are exactly three, and everything else is a variation on one of them.

| Approach | What it is | Cost | Where it breaks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Put it in the prompt | Paste the documents into every request | Tokens on every turn, forever | Stops working when the corpus outgrows a request |
| Retrieve it | Find the relevant pieces first, send only those | An index to build and keep fresh | Send the wrong pieces and you get a confident wrong answer |
| Fine tune | Train the model further on your data | A training run, a dataset, and a repeat every time the data changes | Teaches form well and facts badly |

The first is not a joke. If your knowledge base is a two page policy document that changes
twice a year, paste it into the system prompt and stop reading. Retrieval is machinery, and
machinery you do not need is a liability. The honest threshold is roughly: when the material
you might need no longer fits comfortably in a request, or when most of what you would paste is
irrelevant to most questions, retrieval starts paying for itself.

Fine tuning is the one people reach for by mistake. It is genuinely good at teaching a model a
format, a house style, a classification scheme, a way of structuring an answer. It is poor at
teaching facts. A fine tuned model does not gain a lookup table; it gains a stronger tendency
to produce text that looks like the training set, and text that looks like your documentation
is not the same thing as text that is true. Worse, it produces that text with no way to say
where it came from, and updating a single fact means another training run. You will still see
fine tuning recommended for knowledge injection. Treat that recommendation with suspicion, and
ask what happens when a price changes.

## The long context argument

The obvious objection in 2026 is that context windows are enormous now, so why not skip the
index and send everything. It is a real option, more often than it was two years ago, and
sometimes it is the right one. Three things stop it being the answer in general.

Cost, first, and it is per turn. A prompt is priced by what is in it, so a hundred pages of
context is not a one time expense: it is charged again on every request, forever, whether or
not the answer needed page ninety. Prompt caching cuts this substantially where a provider
offers it and where the long part of your prompt is stable, which makes the arithmetic better
without changing its shape.

Latency, second. Time to first token grows with the size of the prompt. For a batch job nobody
cares. For a chat interface where a person is watching a cursor blink, a few extra seconds on
every turn is the difference between a product people use and one they close.

Attention, third, and this is the one that surprises people. A model that can accept a hundred
pages does not attend to those pages evenly. Retrieval evaluations across long contexts have
found consistently that a fact placed in the middle of a very long prompt is recalled less
reliably than the same fact near the beginning or the end, and that accuracy degrades as the
prompt grows even when the fact is present. The size of the effect depends heavily on the model
and the task, and models have improved at this, so do not treat it as a fixed law. Treat it as
a reason not to assume that "it fits" means "it is read".

## What retrieval actually buys

Two things, and it is worth being precise about them because everything else attributed to RAG
is downstream of these.

A bounded prompt. You decide how much context goes into a request rather than letting the size
of your corpus decide it. That makes cost and latency predictable, and it makes them stay
predictable when the corpus triples.

A citation. This is the bigger one. Because your code chose the passages, your code knows what
they were. That means an answer can be shown next to the text it was drawn from, a reviewer can
check it in seconds, and an automated check can ask whether the answer is supported by anything
that was actually sent. Neither a long prompt nor a fine tuned model gives you that. The long
prompt gives you a haystack and the fine tuned model gives you nothing at all.

Notice what retrieval does not buy. It does not make the model truthful. It does not stop
fabrication. It narrows what the model is working from and it gives you something to check the
answer against, which is a great deal, and it is not the same as a guarantee.

## The failure this module is really about

Here is the shape of the problem, and it is worth keeping in front of you for the next four
lessons.

Your retriever returns three chunks. They are the three that scored highest, and none of them
answers the question, because the document that does answer it phrases things differently, or
was split so the answer straddles two chunks, or was never ingested at all. The model receives
three plausible passages and a question. It answers. The answer is fluent, it is grounded in
the text it was given, it cites source two, and it is wrong.

Nothing in that sequence looks like an error. There is no exception, no empty result, no
warning in a log. The citation, which was supposed to be the safety feature, is now the thing
that makes the wrong answer credible.

Every design decision in the rest of this module points at that failure. Chunking is about
whether the answer survives being split. Embeddings and thresholds are about whether the
retriever can tell a real match from the least bad one. The pipeline lesson is about making the
system decline instead of guessing. And the last lesson is about the other kind of memory, the
conversation itself, where the equivalent failure is quieter: an agent that has silently
forgotten what it agreed to four turns ago and answers confidently anyway.

Start with the split, because it happens first and everything downstream inherits it.
