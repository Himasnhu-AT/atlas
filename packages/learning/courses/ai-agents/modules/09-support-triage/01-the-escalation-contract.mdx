A support agent is the most common agent in production and the one most often built badly.
The reason is that it looks like a retrieval problem. Point a model at the help centre,
answer questions, count the tickets you did not pay a person to read. Two weeks after launch
the queue is full of customers writing "AGENT" in capital letters, and the answers that did
land are the ones nobody needed.

The interesting part of a support agent is not the answering. It is the boundary: knowing
what it does not know, and knowing when the conversation stopped being its job.

## What the numbers actually measure

Every vendor in this space quotes a deflection rate, and it is always higher than the number
that describes your product. The gap is not dishonesty, it is a definition.

Deflection is normally counted as sessions that ended without a ticket being created. A
customer who asked twice, got nothing useful, and closed the tab counts as deflected. So does
one who was answered confidently and incorrectly and will be back on Thursday with a worse
mood. Resolution is a different measurement and a much harder one, because it needs either a
follow up or a human reading the transcript.

A support lead watches four numbers. Deflection, sessions closed without a human, which is easy
to game and worth watching anyway. Resolution, sessions where the customer's problem actually
went away, which is costly to measure and the only one that matters commercially.

Then the two that describe the boundary. Escalation quality, meaning whether the human who
picked the conversation up had to start over. And time to human, meaning how long a customer
who needed a person spent being offered help articles.

The last two are where an agent earns or destroys its keep, and they are the two nobody
instruments, because they need the transcript and not just the outcome. That is what the third
exercise in this module builds.

## The two failure modes

There are exactly two ways to get the boundary wrong, and they pull against each other.

Escalating too early is expensive and invisible. The agent hits an unfamiliar phrasing, gives
up, and hands a person a question the knowledge base answers in one line. Nobody complains,
because the customer got help. It just costs what it always cost, and the project quietly
fails to pay for itself.

Refusing too long is cheap and extremely visible. The customer asks for a person, the agent
offers another article, the customer asks again, the agent offers another article. This is
the interaction that produces the screenshots. It is worse than having no agent at all,
because a contact form does not argue.

An agent tuned only against deflection will always drift toward the second failure. It is the
one that makes the number go up. So the rubric has to punish it explicitly, and it has to
punish the first failure too, or you have just built a very expensive keyword router.

## The contract

Write the behaviour down before writing the agent. This is the contract for what you are
about to build, and every part of it is enforced by a test in the exercises.

Answer only from retrieved context. Every factual sentence carries a bracket id pointing at
the article it came from. A reply that cites nothing is a failure, not an answer, whatever
it says.

When retrieval comes back empty, do not call the model. You already know the answer cannot be
grounded, so the call can only produce something plausible and unsupported, and you will pay
for it.

Out of scope is a route, not a refusal. "I cannot help with that" is the end of the road.
"Nothing here covers this, passing it to the support queue" is a next step.

When the customer asks for a person, push back exactly once. Once is a genuine attempt at the
answer they came for. Twice is arguing.

When you hand over, hand over the work. The summary, everything already tried, and the
articles already cited. A handoff without those makes the customer explain themselves twice,
which is the single most reliable way to turn a neutral interaction into a bad one.

Once a human owns the conversation, stop. No more model calls, no more suggestions.

## What you are building

Three slices, each one a working piece of the same artefact.

The first is the answering layer: retrieve, build the prompt, call the model once, and check
what came back against what was retrieved. It returns a `Reply` carrying the text, the
citations that were real, the citations the model invented, and a route.

The second is the session. It wraps the answering layer in the conversation rules above:
recognising a request for a person, pushing back once, building the `Handoff`, and going
quiet afterwards.

The third is the harness. Personas designed to be difficult, a rubric of five rules a support
lead would recognise, and a suite that fails the build. It grades two deliberately broken
agents as well as the correct one, because a rubric that only ever sees correct behaviour is
not a rubric, it is a fixture.

## What is deliberately not here

No ticket system, no CRM, no live chat transport. Those are integrations, and they are the
easy part: a support platform's API is a POST with a JSON body and you have written a hundred
of those.

No fine tuning and no reranker. Both are real techniques and both are what you reach for
after the boundary works. An agent with excellent retrieval and no escalation contract is
still the agent people screenshot.

No sentiment classifier deciding when to escalate. It is the first thing everyone builds and
it is the wrong shape. Sentiment tells you the customer is angry. It does not tell you the
knowledge base cannot help them, which is the fact you need. An angry customer whose question
is answered by article A2 wants article A2, delivered without a fight.
