Reflection asks *"what is wrong with this answer?"* and gets a critique shaped by the answer.
Chain of verification asks a different question entirely: it takes the answer apart, and asks
about each piece as though it had never seen the rest.

Four steps.

**Decompose.** Split the draft into individual factual claims. One assertion each.

**Question.** Turn each claim into a question that could be answered without knowing the claim.

**Answer independently.** Answer each question in a fresh context. This is the step that does
the work, and it is the step people skip.

**Reconcile.** Compare each independent answer to the original claim, and rewrite the draft
around whatever survived.

## The independence is in the prompt, not the pattern

You can implement all four steps and get nothing, and the way that happens is including the
draft in the verification call.

```python
# Does nothing. The claim is right there.
verify(f"Draft: {draft}\n\nIs this claim true: {claim}?")

# Does the work. The model answers the question cold.
verify(f"{question}")
```

The second one has no draft, no other claims, and no reasoning. If the answer comes back
different from the claim, that difference is information, because the two were produced from
different inputs.

This is the same argument as the previous module's guardrail and the same argument as the last
lesson, and it keeps being the same argument: **a check is worth what it did not read.**

## Decomposition is the hard part

The pattern's reputation rests on step one, and step one is fiddly.

**One assertion per claim.** *"The 2019 model was released in March and sold 40,000 units"* is
two claims, and verifying it as one gives you a single verdict on a sentence that could be half
right. Split on the conjunction.

**Claims must stand alone.** *"It was discontinued the following year"* cannot be checked, because
"it" and "following" are relative to the sentence before. Resolve the references during
decomposition or the verification question is unanswerable.

**Not everything is a claim.** *"This is a significant improvement"* is a judgement, and running
it through a fact check produces a confident-sounding verdict on an opinion. Separate the
checkable from the not-checkable, and only verify the first kind.

That third one is where most implementations go wrong. A verification pass that returns
`verified: true` on *"the results are impressive"* has taught you nothing and looks like it
taught you something.

## Reconciling

Each claim comes back supported, contradicted, or unsupported, and those three want different
treatment.

**Supported** stays.

**Contradicted** — the independent answer says something different — gets corrected or removed.
Correcting is better when you trust the independent answer; removing is safer when you do not.

**Unsupported** — the independent answer did not know — is the one people get wrong. It is not
the same as contradicted. The claim may be perfectly true and simply outside what the
verification could reach. Deleting it silently loses good content; keeping it silently keeps a
claim you failed to check. Mark it and let the caller decide, which is the same shape as the
quarantine in the data engineering course:
[quarantine, not deletion](lesson:de-cleaning.quarantine-not-deletion).

## Where you have already seen this

[The verification pass](lesson:ai-research-briefing.the-verification-pass) in the research
briefing project is this pattern applied to citations: every factual sentence is checked against
the source text it claims to come from, and anything that fails does not ship.

That project version has an advantage this one does not — the source text is right there, so
the check is against a document rather than against another model call. When you have a source,
use it. Model-answers-question is the fallback for when you do not, and it is weaker.

## What the exercise asks

Decompose a draft into claims, separate the checkable ones from the judgements, generate a
verification question for each, answer them in fresh contexts, and reconcile into a final
answer with the three verdicts kept distinct.

The test worth reading twice checks the independence precisely, because the loose version of the
claim is wrong. The claim under test **has** to be in its own verification prompt, or there is
no question to ask. What must not be there is the rest of the draft: the other claims, and the
reasoning that produced them. That is the whole pattern, and it is one line to get wrong.
