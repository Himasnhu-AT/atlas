[The RAG pipeline](lesson:ai-memory-and-rag.the-rag-pipeline) does the same four things every
time: embed the question, take the top k, put them in the prompt, answer. It is the right thing
to build first and it has two assumptions in it that are not always true.

It assumes the top k are relevant. Similarity search always returns something; on a question
your corpus does not cover, it returns the least irrelevant chunks with no signal that this is
what happened.

And it assumes retrieval was needed at all. *"Thanks, that helped"* triggers an embedding call
and stuffs three chunks of documentation into the context of a message that needed neither.

Everything in this lesson is the module's one idea — check the thing that produced the answer,
using something independent of it — pointed at retrieval. The difference is that here the
correction can succeed, because unlike a model thinking harder, a retriever can go and get the
missing fact.

## Grade what came back

The fix for the first assumption is a step between retrieval and generation: for each retrieved
chunk, does it actually help answer this question?

That grade is per chunk, not per result set, and it is a small independent judgement — the chunk
and the question, nothing else. Three outcomes:

**Relevant.** Keep it.

**Not relevant.** Drop it. A chunk that scored well on embedding similarity and does not answer
the question is noise, and noise in the context makes the answer worse rather than merely not
better.

**Ambiguous.** Keep it and mark it, exactly like the unsupported claims in
[chain of verification](lesson:ai-self-correction.chain-of-verification).

The interesting case is when everything is graded not relevant. That is the pipeline
discovering that its corpus does not cover the question, which the plain version could never
find out.

## Then do something about it

Knowing the retrieval failed is only useful if there is a next move.

**Re-query.** The commonest reason for a bad result set is that the question is not phrased the
way the documents are. A user asks *"why did my card get declined"*; the documentation says
*"error 402: payment method rejected"*. Rewriting the query into the corpus's vocabulary and
searching again is cheap and often works.

**Widen.** Take more chunks, or drop a filter that was excluding the right document.

**Say you do not know.** The answer nobody implements, and the correct one when the corpus
genuinely lacks the fact. It is much better than the alternative, which is answering anyway from
whatever was in the context. The support project makes this a rule in
[grounded answers and the scope boundary](lesson:ai-support-triage.grounded-answers-and-scope);
this is the retrieval-side machinery that lets you honour it.

Bound the re-querying. Two attempts, then stop. A loop that keeps rewriting until it finds
something will eventually find something, and what it finds will be whatever matched the query
it drifted into.

## Decide whether to retrieve

The second assumption. Not every message needs the corpus.

A cheap classification before retrieving — does answering this need documents we hold? — skips
the whole pipeline for conversational turns, for questions answerable from the conversation so
far, and for anything the model plainly knows.

Two things this buys beyond latency. It stops irrelevant chunks landing in the context of a
message that did not need them, which is a real quality improvement and not just a saving. And
it makes the retrieval that does happen more legible in a trace, because a retrieval call now
means something decided it was necessary.

The risk is skipping when you should not have, and it points one way: **when unsure, retrieve.**
An unnecessary retrieval costs tokens. A skipped necessary one costs a wrong answer delivered
confidently.

## The three names

These get sold as three architectures. They are three decisions.

Grading results and re-querying on failure is usually called **corrective RAG**. Deciding
whether to retrieve at all is **adaptive RAG**. Having the generation step also judge whether
its own answer is supported by what it retrieved is **self-RAG**, and that last one is chain of
verification with the retrieved chunks as the source.

Worth knowing the words because papers and libraries use them. Not worth treating as three
things to choose between: they compose, and a real pipeline usually wants all three, each of
them a few lines around the pipeline you already have.

## What the exercise asks

A relevance grader, a query rewriter, a bounded corrective loop that re-queries and gives up
honestly, and the decision about whether to retrieve at all.

The test to read is the one where the corpus does not contain the answer: the assertion is that
the pipeline says so, rather than answering from three chunks that scored well and were about
something else.
